                                             ON THE IRRATIONALITY EXPONENT OF REAL NUMBERS
                                                     WITH LOW COMPLEXITY EXPANSION


                                                        YANN BUGEAUD, HAJIME KANEKO, AND DONG HAN KIM
arXiv:2510.17177v3 [math.NT] 20 Mar 2026




                                                   Abstract. Let ξ be a real number and b ≥ 2 an integer. We study the rela-
                                                   tionship between the irrationality exponent of ξ and the subword complexity
                                                   p(n, x) of the b-ary expansion x of ξ, where p(n, x) counts the number of dis-
                                                   tinct blocks of length n in x, for n ≥ 1. If the irrationality exponent of ξ
                                                   is equal to 2, which is the case for almost all real numbers ξ, we show that
                                                   the limit superior of the sequence (p(n, x)/n)n≥1 is at least equal to 4/3. The
                                                   proof is based on a careful study of the evolution of the Rauzy graphs of infinite
                                                   words of low complexity.

Classical references on combinatorics on words and on Sturmian sequences include
[6,15,17]. The words x for which there exists an integer C such that p(n, x) ≤ n+C
for n ≥ 0 are called quasi-Sturmian. See [11] for more details.
    Let ξ be a real number. Let b be an integer greater than or equal to 2. There
exists a unique infinite sequence (xj )j≥1 of integers from {0, 1, . . . , b − 1}, called the
b-ary expansion of ξ, such that

A natural way to measure the complexity of the real number ξ written in base b
as in (1.2) is to count the number of distinct blocks of given length in the infinite
word x = x1 x2 . . .. We set

                               p(n, ξ, b) = p(n, x),    n ≥ 1,

and call the function n 7→ p(n, ξ, b) the complexity function of ξ in base b.
    Adamczewski [3] has observed that the statements on the combinatorial structure
of Sturmian words established in [7] and [4] almost immediately imply that the
irrationality exponent of any real number, whose sequence of digits in some integer
base is quasi-Sturmian, is greater than 2. The main result of [8] asserts that, if the
complexity function of ξ in base b grows sufficiently slowly, then the sequence of
digits of ξ contains very large repetitions, that causes the irrationality exponent of
ξ to exceed 2.

     Let x denote the word given by the b-ary expansion of an irrational, real number
ξ. In [8,10], we have shown that there are δ, δ ′ , δ ′′ > 0 such that, if r(n, x) ≤ (1+δ)n
for every sufficiently large integer n, then there are arbitrarily large n such that
r(n, x) ≤ (1 − δ ′ )n (see [8, Theorem 3.5], [10, Proposition 1.6]), thus, by [8, Lemma
3.6], we have µ(ξ) ≥ 2 + δ ′′ . Said differently, if µ(ξ) < 2 + δ ′′ , then there are
arbitrarily large n such that p(n, x) ≥ r(n, x) ≥ (1 + δ)n.
     In the present paper, our approach is different. Our assumption is on the function
n 7→ p(n, x) and not on the function n 7→ r(n, x). We show that there are δ, δ ′ > 0
such that, if p(n, x) ≤ (1 + δ)n for every sufficiently large integer n, then, there are
arbitrarily large n such that r(n, x) ≤ (1 − δ ′ )n.
     To achieve this, we study very carefully the combinatorial structure of words
of small complexity, focusing on the evolution of their Rauzy graphs defined in
   IRRATIONALITY EXPONENT OF NUMBERS WITH LOW COMPLEXITY EXPANSION                    5


Subsection 2.1. Previous works on these words and their Rauzy graphs include
[1,2], where Aberkane studies, among other, the infinite words x such that p(n, x)/n
tends to 1 as n tends to infinity. Also, Heinis [16] established that if the sequence
(p(n, x)/n)n≥1 has a limit, then this limit cannot be in the open interval (1, 2). This
interval is the best one can obtain, since there exist sequences x with p(n, x) = 2n+1
for n ≥ 1. Certain such sequences, under the additional assumption of minimality,
have been studied by Arnoux and Rauzy [5], again by means of the evolution of
their Rauzy graphs. Recently, Creutz and Pavlov [13] showed that if X is an
infinite transitive subshift with lim sup p(n)/n < 4/3, where p(n) is the number of
n-subwords appearing in sequences in the subshift X, then X is uniquely ergodic
and its unique ergodic measure has discrete spectrum. See also [14].
  One important difficulty we had to overcome is that there is no additional as-
sumption on x in Theorem 1.4. In particular, x is not supposed to be minimal, nor
even recurrent: it may happen that arbitrarily long prefixes of x occur only once
in x.

                               2. Auxiliary lemmas

  Throughout the paper, we assume that the words are written over a finite al-
phabet.

2.1. Special words and the Rauzy graph. Let n be a positive integer. An
n-word is a word of length n. If x is an infinite word, an n-subword (or, n-factor)
of x is a factor of x of length n.

Definition 2.1. Let x be an infinite word and n a positive integer. We let Fn (x)
denote the set of n-subwords of x. A word w in Fn (x) is called right-special if
there exist distinct letters x, y such that wx and wy are subwords of x. A word w
in Fn (x) is called left-special if there exist distinct letters x, y such that xw and
yw are subwords of x. Any word in Fn (x) which is right-special and left-special is
called a bi-special word.

  The following lemma is an immediate consequence of Definition 2.1.

Lemma 2.2. Let x be an infinite word and m, n integers with n > m ≥ 1.
  (i) If w is a left-special n-word of x, then the prefix of w of length m is a left
special m-word of x.
  (ii) If w is a right-special n-word of x, then the suffix of w of length m is a right
special m-word of x.

Lemma 2.3. Suppose that x is a non-ultimately periodic word. Then there is at
least one left-special word w with the property that there are two distinct letters x, y
such that the words xw and yw occur infinitely many times in x.

Proof. If there is no such left-special word, then after deleting a suitable prefix of
x the remaining word has no left-special words, which implies that x is ultimately
periodic by (1.1).                                                                          □

    Let n be a positive integer. We define the Rauzy graph Gn = Gn (x) of x. The
set of vertices of the oriented graph Gn is the set of subwords of x of length n.
There exists an edge from u to v in Gn if and only if there exist letters x, y such
that ux = yv is a subword of x. In this case, we write

                                      [uv] = ux = yv.

A finite sequence of edges [u1 u2 ], [u2 u3 ], . . . , [uk−1 uk ] is called a path and we write
it as [u1 u2 . . . uk−1 uk ]. A subword of length n + ℓ of the word x induces a path of
length ℓ in Gn . Moreover, a path of length ℓ in Gn+k induces a path of length ℓ + k
in Gn . However, the converse does not hold in general.
    Let U and V be two paths in Gn . If the last vertex of U and the initial vertex of V
are identical, we define the concatenated path U V in a natural way. We denote the
length of a path U in Gn by |U |n . For example, we have |U |n = k and |U | = n + k,
where | · | denotes the length (that is, the number of letters) of a word.
    In the Rauzy graph, any non-right special vertex has exactly one outgoing edge,
and any non-left special vertex has exactly one incoming edge, unless it is a prefix
of x having no other occurrence in x. Any right special vertex has at least two
outgoing edges. Any left special vertex has at least two incoming edges.

2.2. Recurrent words.

Definition 2.4. A subword w of an infinite word x is recurrent if w occurs in x
infinitely many times. An infinite word x is n-recurrent if every subword of x of
length n is recurrent. An infinite word x is called recurrent if every subword of x
is recurrent.

x = an zn and zn is n-recurrent. We call an the n-th non-recurrent prefix of x
and Gn′ (x) := Gn (zn+1 ) the n-th reduced Rauzy graph of x, which is a subgraph

Lemma 2.8. Let x = x1 x2 . . . be a non-ultimately periodic word. Let n ≥ 1.
Suppose that p(n + 1, x) = p(n, x) + 1. Then there is one right-special word of
length n. Moreover, if x1 . . . xn occurs in x more than once, then there is a unique
left-special word of length n in x and x is n-recurrent.

Definition 2.9. A right-special word w is called essential if w is a suffix of a
right-special word of any length (or of arbitrarily large length, this is equivalent).

    From Lemma 2.2, we note that for any non-essential right-special word w there
exists m such that w is not a suffix of a right special word of length bigger than m.
Therefore, if x is not ultimately periodic, then for each n there exists an essential
right-special word of length n.

Lemma 2.10. Suppose that lim inf n→∞ p(n, x)/n < 2 and x is not ultimately
periodic. Then, for each n, there exists a unique essential right-special word of
length n.

Proof. By the above remark, there exists an essential right-special word of length n.
By assumption, there is an integer m > n such that p(m + 1, x) = p(m, x) + 1. By
Lemma 2.8, there exists one right-special word of length m. Therefore, the number
of essential right-special words of length n is at most one.                          □

Lemma 2.11. Suppose that x is n-recurrent and non-ultimately periodic. If p(n +
1, x) = p(n, x) + 1, then x is (n + 1)-recurrent.

that a is non-empty. Let z = xs+1 xs+2 . . . . Then xs+1 . . . xs+n is a left-special n-

the choice of a and z.                                                                □
