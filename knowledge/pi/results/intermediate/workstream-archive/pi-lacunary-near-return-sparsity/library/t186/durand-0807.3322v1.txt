                                          Abstract

                                          We prove that a sequence is primitive substitutive if and only if the set of its derived
                                          sequences is finite; we defined these sequences here.




                                          1    Introduction


                                          The purpose of this paper is to characterize primitive substitutive sequences,
                                          i.e. sequences defined as the image, under a morphism, of a fixed point of
                                          a primitive substitution. No general characterizations of these sequences are
                                          known. In this paper we give one, it uses a notion we introduce here, the return
                                          word . We don’t know reference or study about it, except in the area of sym-
                                          bolic dynamical systems, where it is closely related to induced transformations
                                          ([7,11]). But no combinatoric results are obtained.

                                          For some classes of sequences arising from substitutions characterizations ex-
                                          ist. The first class appearing in the literature is the class of the sequences
                                          generated by q-substitutions, also called uniform tag sequences ([6]). These
                                          are images, under a ”letter to letter” morphism, of a fixed point of a substitu-
                                          tion of constant length q. A. Cobham [6] proved that a sequence is generated
                                          by a q-substitution if and only if it is q-automatic, i.e. if it is generated by
                                          a q-automaton (see also [8]). When q is a prime power, an algebraic char-
                                          acterization is given by G. Christol, T. Kamae, M. Mendès France and G.
                                          Rauzy [5]. And S. Fabre [9] generalized the theorem of A. Cobham to a small
                                          class of substitutions of non-constant length, by introducing the concept of
                                          Θ-automaton and Θ-substitution.

                                          It turns out that our characterization is similar to the following known one: a
                                          sequence X = (Xn )n∈IN is generated by a q-substitution if and only if the set
                                          of the sequences (Xqk n+a )n∈IN , where 0 ≤ a ≤ q k − 1 and k ≥ 0, is finite ([8]).

Our main tool of this paper is the return word over u, where u is a non-
empty prefix of a minimal sequence X. It is defined as a word separating two
successive factors of u. We introduce a ”coding” of X with the return words
over u and obtain an other minimal sequence Du (X) we call derived sequence
of X. The main result can be formulated as follows: a sequence is primitive
substitutive if and only if the set of derived sequences is finite.

The paper is divided into six sections. The next section contains the defini-
tions, the notation and the basic results needed to state the main theorem.
The third section shows that the condition of the characterization is sufficient.
In the following one, we extend a result, useful for the proof of the main the-
orem, due to B. Mossé [10], concerning the ”power of words”, to primitive
substitutive sequences and we deduce some return word properties of these
sequences. In the fifth section we prove the main theorem, considering first
the case of the fixed point, and then the case of its image by a morphism. The
last one deals with perspectives and open questions.



2   Definitions, notations


2.1 Words and sequences


Let us recall some standard notations we can find in [12]. We call alphabet
a finite set of elements called letters; symbols A and B will always denote
alphabets. A word on A of length n is an element x = x0 x1 · · · xn−1 of An ,
|x| = n is the length of x and ∅ denotes the empty-word of length 0. The set of
non-empty words on A is denoted by A+ , and A∗ = A+ ∪ {∅}. If S is a subset
of A+ , we denote S + the set of words which can be written as a concatenation
of elements of S, and S ∗ = S + ∪ {∅}. A sequence on A is an element of AIN .
If X = X0 X1 · · · is a sequence, and, l and k are two non-negative integers,
with l ≥ k, we write X[k,l] the word Xk Xk+1 · · · Xl ; a word u is a factor of X
if there exists k ≤ l with X[k,l] = u; if u = X[0,l] for some l, we say that u is
a prefix of X and we write u ≺ X. The empty-word is a prefix of X. The set
of factors of length n is written Ln (X), and the set of factors of X by L(X).
If u is a factor of X, we will call occurrence of u in X every integer i such
that X[i,i+|u|−1] = u, i.e. such that u is a prefix of X[i,+∞]. The sequence X is
ultimately periodic if there exist a word u and a word v such that X = uv ω ,
where v ω is the infinite concatenation of the word v. It is periodic if u is the
empty-word. We will call A(X) the set of letters occurring in X.

If X is a word, we use the same terminology with the similar definitions. A
word u is a suffix of the word X if |u| ≤ |X| and u = X[|X|−|u|,|X|−1]. The
empty-word is a suffix of X.

2.2 Substitutions and substitutive sequences


In this part we give a definition of what we call substitution. In the literature
we can find the notions of iterated morphism ([3]), tag system ([6]) and CDOL
system ([13]). They are similars to the notion of substitution.

Definition 1 A substitution is a triple ζ = (ζ, A, α), where A is an alphabet,
ζ a map from A to A+ and α an element of A such that:

(1) the first letter of ζ(α) is α,
(2) |ζ n (α)| → +∞, when n → +∞.

This definition could be more general but it is not very restrictive. Indeed,
as we study sequences generated by morphisms we need at least one letter
satisfying the condition 2. Among these letters there is always one, β, such
that β and a power of ζ satisfy the condition 1. Details can be found in [12].

The sequence limi→+∞ ζ i (α) exists (see [12]) and is called fixed point of ζ =
(ζ, A, α), we will denote it Xζ ; it is characterized by ζ(Xζ ) = Xζ and α is the
first letter of Xζ .

Definition 2 We say that a substitution ζ = (ζ, A, α) is primitive if there
exists an integer k such that for all elements β and γ in A, γ is a factor of
ζ k (β).

If ζ = (ζ, A, α) is primitive then for all β belonging to A we have |ζ n (β)| →
+∞, when n tends to infinity.

Definition 3 Let X be a sequence on A. The sequence X is minimal (or
uniformly recurrent) if for every integer l there exists an integer k such that
each word of Ll (X) occurs in every word of length k.

Equivalently, a sequence X is minimal if for every non-empty factor u of X,
the maximal difference between two successive occurrences of u is bounded.
If ζ is primitive then the fixed point Xζ is minimal (see [12]). But there exist
non-primitive substitutions with minimal fixed points. Let ζ = (ζ, {1, 2, 3}, 1)
be the substitution defined by ζ(1) = 123, ζ(2) = 2 and ζ(3) = 13. It is not
primitive but its fixed point is minimal.

Definition 4 A sequence Y over the alphabet A is called substitutive if there
exist a substitution ζ = (ζ, B, α) and a map ϕ : B → A such that ϕ(Xζ ) = Y ,


                                       3
and substitutive primitive when ζ is primitive.

It is easy to check that a substitutive primitive sequence is minimal.

3   The condition is sufficient


To prove that ”if the number of the different derived sequences of a minimal
sequence X is finite then X is substitutive primitive”. We need the following
proposition. It states that the image of a minimal fixed point by a morphism
from A to B+ is a substitutive primitive sequence.

Now we are able to show that the condition of the characterization is sufficient.

Theorem 12 If the minimal sequence X has a finite number of derived se-
quences, then it is substitutive primitive.


PROOF. If X is periodic, then it is easy to check that X is the fixed point
of a primitive substitution of constant length.

Let X be non-periodic. There exists a prefix u of X such that the set K =
{v ≺ X; Dv (X) = Du (X)} is infinite. By minimality, we can choose n so large
that every factor of length n of X contains each element of HX,u as a factor.
By Lemma 10 there exists a word v belonging to K such that |w| ≥ n for
all w belonging to HX,v . With u and v the hypothesis of Proposition 11 are
fulfilled, this completes the proof.

Dv (Xζ ) = 1ω for all words v such that u is a prefix of v and v is a prefix of
Xζ . The proof is completed.

6   Open problems and perspectives


6.1 Topological dynamical system


Let X be a minimal sequence and Ω = ({T n X; n ∈ IN}, T ), where T is the
shift, be the dynamical system generated by X (see [7,11] or [12]). In dynamical
topological terms, we can formulate Theorem 5 as follows: X is primitive
substitutive if and only if the number of induced systems on cylinder generated
by a prefix is finite. We don’t know whether it is true if we induce on any
cylinders, or on any clopen sets.

Our method characterizes only one point of Ω, is it possible to characterize
all the points belonging to Ω by analysis methods?


                                      13
6.2 Minimality and primitivity


Our method allows us to characterize the primitive substitutive sequences,
but we do not know if every substitutive minimal sequence is primitive, i.e.
generated by a primitive substitution. We can only prove this for the minimal
fixed points arising from non-primitive substitutions. The following example
is significant and give a sketch of the proof.

Let ζ = (ζ, {1, 2}, 1) be the non-primitive substitution defined by ζ(1) = 1211
et ζ(2) = 2. The sequence Xζ is minimal. There exists a prefix u of Xζ such
that Du (Xζ ) is the fixed point of the primitive substitution τXζ ,u . Then Xζ is
primitive substitutive because ΘXζ ,u (Du (Xζ )) = Xζ . The sequence Xζ is the
image under ϕ : {1, 2, 3} → {1, 2}, defined by ϕ(1) = ϕ(3) = 1 and ϕ(2) = 2,
of the fixed point of the primitive substitution τ = (τ, {1, 2, 3}, 1), defined by
τ (1) = 12, τ (2) = 312 and τ (3) = 1233. The proof of Theorem 9 gives us the
morphisms ϕ and τ .

It remains to treat the case of the substitutive minimal sequences arising from
sequences which are not minimals.




6.3 Complexity, return words and S-adic sequences


B. Mossé recently proved that if X is the fixed point of a primitive substitution
then the sequence (pX (n + 1) − pX (n))n∈IN is bounded, where pX (n) is the
number of the words of length n belonging to L(X). With our results we
are able to give a short proof of this property, and to extend it to primitive
substitutive sequences. Indeed we can prove, with the help of the graph of
words of Rauzy [1], the following property:
if Y is a minimal sequence such that (Card(HY,Y[0,n] ))n∈IN is bounded then
(pY (n + 1) − pY (n))n∈IN is bounded.

A S-adic sequence Y on the alphabet A is given by an infinite product of
substitutions belonging to a finite set S. B. Host formulated the following
conjecture: a minimal sequence Y is S-adic if and only if (pY (n+1)−pY (n))n∈IN
is bounded.

If the matrices of the substitutions of S (see [12]) are strictly positives then we
can prove that (Card(HY,Y[0,n] ))n∈IN is bounded, by extending the proof of the
primitive substitutive case. It follows that (pY (n + 1) − pY (n))n∈IN is bounded.
